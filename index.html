The big picture: money left AI and came to India

One shift ties the day together: investors are moving away from AI-linked assets and towards cheaper, steadier markets.

In short
  • Foreign funds bought over ₹1 lakh crore of Indian shares in November, the most ever.
  • At the same time they sold technology shares worldwide at the fastest pace in about 20 years.
  • Falling metal and oil prices help India, but US interest rates are still rising.

A rotation, not a crash

Global fund managers cut their technology holdings by the most since 2006. In the US, defensive shares such as food, health and power companies beat the tech-heavy Nasdaq index by about 23 points over 2 months. Some of that money found its way to India, which has few AI companies and so looks like a safe place to hide.

One view is that this is the start of a long return to India after 26 months of foreign selling. The opposite view points to the futures market, where foreign funds still hold 68% of their index bets on a fall, so part of the cash buying may be a hedged trade that can reverse fast.

Cheap commodities, costly money

Copper fell to a 7-month low and aluminium dropped below $2,900 a tonne. Crude oil is down about a fifth in November. For India, which imports most of its oil and many metals, this is good news: car makers, air-conditioner makers and paint companies pay less for their inputs.

The catch is the price of money. US inflation is still high, almost every Fed official sees more rate rises, and the US 10-year borrowing cost is near 5%. India's own inflation hit 6.09%, so the Reserve Bank has less room to help.

Reading between the lines

The same AI boom that lifted US shares is now pushing up US inflation. Fed officials said so in their minutes: data-centre building is straining supply. So the AI trade hurts India twice and helps once. It keeps US rates high, it drains money from Asian chip markets, but it also sends nervous money looking for markets without AI risk, and India is the biggest of those.

So the winners right now are Indian large companies that gain from cheaper inputs, mainly car makers, banks and consumer firms. The losers are miners, gold-linked companies and smaller Indian shares, which local funds are selling to meet redemptions.

02

The price of money: the Fed hikes again and Indian inflation crosses 6%

US rates, India's inflation, the rupee and gold, and why they now point in different directions.

In short
  • Almost every US Fed official expects to raise rates again, probably in December.
  • India's retail inflation rose to 6.09%, above the Reserve Bank's upper limit.
  • Gold and silver fell hard as the dollar rose to a 19-month high.

The Fed is not done

The minutes of the Fed's October meeting (the written record of its discussion) showed that almost all officials see more rate rises ahead. A couple said December could bring a rise bigger than 0.25%. Many named the AI building boom as a real source of inflation, because it is pushing up prices for power, chips and labour.

  • Market pricing — interest-rate futures give a 108.6% chance of a December rise, so a hike is fully expected.
  • JPMorgan — now expects rises at each of the next 3 meetings.
  • US 10-year yield — about 5.07% (a yield is what lenders demand to lend for that long); the 20-year auction cleared at 5.459%.
  • Jobs — weekly jobless claims rose to 203,000, above the 197,000 expected.
  • Debt load — about $8 trillion of US government debt must be refinanced in 12 months at higher rates.

One view is that the US cannot live with rates this high and will end up printing money to protect its bond market. The opposite view, held by some bond traders, is that the 10-year yield has peaked for now and will drift lower in the near term.

India's inflation problem

Retail inflation rose to 6.09% in October, and food prices were up 8.94% from a year ago. The Reserve Bank had already raised its repo rate (the rate at which it lends to banks) to 5.50% on 7 October, its first rise in years.

A rupee that is getting stronger

The rupee has gained as foreign money arrived. It closed at 93.51 per dollar on 17 November, its strongest since April, after hitting a record low of 96.96 in May.

03

Oil, ships and war: the barrel is cheaper, the danger is not

Crude and fuel margins fell sharply even as tankers burn near Hormuz and talk of new strikes on Iran grows.

In short
  • Brent crude fell to the low $90s, down about a fifth in November.
  • Refining margins, which had pushed up diesel prices, collapsed even faster.
  • Yet attacks on ships and talk of new US strikes on Iran keep the risk high.

Fuel got cheaper first

For central banks, the bigger story is not the crude price but the refining margin, which is the gap between the price of crude and the fuels made from it. That margin had hit records from July to September and pushed up diesel costs everywhere. It has now fallen back close to pre-war levels.

One view says the market is oversupplied: US output is at a record, stocks are rising and the IEA (the International Energy Agency) sees a surplus. The opposite view notes that Brent's near-month spreads (the price gap between the nearest month's and the next month's oil) did not weaken in the sell-off, which they normally do when a glut is real, and that inventories are very low.

Hormuz is still half shut

The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow sea lane out of the Gulf that normally carries about a fifth of the world's oil. Big tankers are now targets, so smaller ships are doing the work, often moving oil from ship to ship in the Gulf of Oman.

  • Flow — the 7-day average fell to about 7-8 mbpd (million barrels a day), against about 20 in normal times.
  • Attacks — 8 tankers or gas carriers were hit between 10 and 17 November; Iran's Revolutionary Guards said 3 more were on fire.
  • Discounts — Gulf producers are cutting prices by $7-11 a barrel to move cargoes out.
  • Small ships — mid-size tankers moved about 2 mbpd out in October, the most since the war began.
  • Saudi Arabia — Houthi rebels hit the Riyadh tower housing the national wealth fund, the 2nd attack on the city's finance district in November.
  • Yemen — Saudi officials expect the war there to run well into 2027.

Talk of another round on Iran

The New York Times reported that President Trump is weighing another extensive round of strikes on Iran, under pressure from Israel. Israel's military and intelligence chiefs reportedly told Washington that Iran is preparing chemical weapons. Trump has also cut his vice president out of Iran peace talks after the vice president blamed the war for mid-term election losses.

Views split sharply. One view takes the warning seriously, given Iran's rebuilt missiles and drones. The opposite view says no evidence was shown to Gulf states, compares it to the Iraq weapons claims of 2003, and warns that blame is being set up before any event.

3 leaders, 1 vague promise

Presidents Xi, Putin and Trump held their first three-way meeting in Shenzhen during the APEC summit. They pledged, in loose terms, to raise the supply of refined fuels and spoke about avoiding nuclear war. Putin later said there was no deal to make such meetings regular, and Poland's prime minister reacted with "God, protect us from such friends."

Reading between the lines, the pledge changes little: the US is already refining at full stretch, China can only make more fuel by buying more crude, and Russia's refineries are under Ukrainian attack. Some traders had sold oil fearing a secret US-China deal; that fear looks overdone.

So for India the near-term picture is good, with cheaper crude, cheaper diesel and a stronger rupee. But the risk has moved, not gone. A single confirmed loss of a large tanker, or a new US strike, could reverse the price fall within days, and India's cost of crude depends on that sea lane.

A fight over commodity trading

NSE moved the expiry day of its crude oil options away from MCX's, and its share of energy options trading tripled to 3.04%. In natural gas options it crossed half of the market on 1 expiry day. BSE plans to enter from December.

One view says NSE can do to MCX what BSE did to NSE in equity options, and NSE shares rose 18% in November. The opposite view says MCX still holds about 97% of energy options and its monopoly in electricity, crude and gold is hard to break; UBS raised its MCX target to ₹4,500.

Reading between the lines, the foreign buying is a bet on India's big, liquid names, not on the whole market. So until local funds stop selling, index gains will not lift most portfolios, and the companies gaining most are those whose costs are falling.

India's economy and policy: big budgets, thin know-how

Defence, state banks, asset sales, solar and chips: money is flowing, but India still depends on China for the machines.

In short
  • The defence ministry wants 20% more money for equipment next year.
  • State-owned banks posted record profits with the lowest bad loans in their history.
  • Solar, batteries, drones and chips still rely on Chinese machines and parts.

Defence: from budget to orders

The defence ministry has asked for a 20% rise in capital spending next year, after a 22% rise this year. It wants to turn the money into firm orders for Indian makers, and it is rewriting buying rules to favour private companies.

  • AMCA — the 5th-generation stealth fighter will go to a private-led team: Tata Advanced Systems, L&T with BEL and Dynamatic, or Bharat Forge with BEML and Data Patterns.
  • Barak — a ₹1,976 crore deal with Israel Aerospace Industries upgrades the Navy's ship-defence missiles.
  • Theatre commands — the Air Force has 29 fighter squadrons against 42 sanctioned; its new chief says "everybody is on board" for joint commands.
  • Japanese frigates — India is studying Mogami-class ships, but they use American guns and launchers the Navy does not run.
  • Japan — its forces will use an Indian firing range for the first time next year.

One view is that the Air Force's shortage is the best reason for theatre commands, since a single authority can move scarce jets between fronts. The opposite view, long held by the Air Force, is that splitting few squadrons leaves every front too thin.

State banks at a record

Public sector banks earned a record ₹1.09 lakh crore in the first half, up 16.9%. Their bad loans are the lowest in their history, and all of them have set aside money to cover over 90% of those loans.

Solar on every roof, machines from China

The PM Surya Ghar scheme has put solar panels on about 57 lakh roofs, with 19.74 GW working. 3 states hold over half of it.

This hurts power distributors. Large factories in steel, cement and cars now make their own solar and wind power, and they were the customers who paid high tariffs that subsidised homes. So distributors lose their best payers while still serving everyone else.

  • Machines — India can make 233 GW of solar panels a year, but 9 in 10 costly machines are imported and it makes almost no wafers.
  • China — proposed curbs on exports of 12 solar technologies in October; German machines cost 40-50% more.
  • Research — the 5 biggest listed solar makers earned about ₹7,300 crore last year and spent about ₹125 crore on research.
  • Batteries — only about 1-1.4 GWh of the 40 GWh cell scheme is built; China's export rebate on batteries goes to zero on 1 January 2027.
  • Drones — 80-90% of flight controllers are imported; China extended export controls to drone motors and radios from 18 November.

Chips: generous money, missing people

Andhra Pradesh will match the Centre's chip support, offering up to 80% of approved capital costs and a 5,000-acre park at Tirupati. Japanese firms such as Mitsui, AOI Electronics, Renesas and Tokyo Electron are building packaging and equipment links in Gujarat.

The gap is skills. The ₹1,27,500 crore chip plan starts with older, simpler chips, while a cutting-edge 2 nm plant would need hundreds of engineers who have run one, and India has almost none. Micron's Sanand plant is already exporting memory chips, mostly to Malaysia for final testing.

Reading between the lines, the government is good at paying for factories and slow at building know-how. So each new Chinese export rule (batteries, solar tools, drone parts, magnets) lands as a cost shock on Indian makers, and the firms that make the machines and materials, not just the end products, are the ones that will matter.

07

Consumers, services and pharma: cheaper inputs, pricier money

How shoppers, IT firms, jewellers and drug makers are faring as costs fall and AI changes pricing.

In short
  • Consumer firms are getting relief as crude and metals fall, and brokers have turned positive.
  • AI is cutting the prices Indian IT firms can charge, before it cuts the work.
  • Jewellers and gold lenders fell as gold dropped to a 14-month low.

Shoppers and brands

Packaged-goods volumes grew 9% last quarter, the best in several quarters, and margins look to have bottomed as crude eased. Brokers returning from investor meetings report more interest in Indian consumer shares.

Nvidia: great numbers, no founder

Nvidia's revenue doubled to $115.8 billion last quarter and it guided $130 billion for the next, above estimates, plus a much bigger share buyback. But it was the first results call in decades without Jensen Huang, who left after reports of extramarital affairs and a suspected Chinese spy plot.

  • Board — chair Harvey Jones says Huang will not return; a search firm has approached Arm's chief Rene Haas.
  • Shareholders — a petition to bring him back passed 200,000 names, including about 24% of staff; Bill Ackman says the board failed its duty.
  • Stock — about 38% below its high and the most oversold since 2019; Moody's cut its outlook to stable.
  • Staff — interim chief Colette Kress promised early pay rises and equity in December.
  • Financing — a $500 billion plan with big lenders to fund customers' chip purchases has lent nothing; no deal is expected before 2027.

One view, shared by Elon Musk, is that removing Huang was like dropping Michael Jordan mid-season, because he managed suppliers and customers personally. The opposite view is that the business does not depend on one man: data-centre sales still doubled and the roadmap is unchanged.

Problem 1: cheap money is over

Fed officials see the AI building boom itself as a source of inflation, and a few warned that long-run inflation expectations could slip loose after 5 years of high prices. In India, food inflation is near 9%. One view says India's cheaper oil will cool inflation fast. The opposite view says food, not fuel, is driving it, and rain shortfalls make that worse.

Problem 2: the AI debt pile

Fed officials said AI-related debt has grown fast through "complex and opaque" deals, and that circular financing and off-balance-sheet leases could magnify losses if returns disappoint. Signs of strain are already visible.

  • Bank loans — ING and Société Générale are trying to sell up to $800 million of data-centre loans, some tied to Oracle's projects.
  • Software debt — banks have been stuck with over $5 billion of Qualtrics acquisition debt since March.
  • Hedge fund — Situational Awareness fell from over $20 billion to a rescue by Citadel, and is now seeking leverage again.
  • Neoclouds — Apollo's chief warned many small cloud firms could lose access to capital without guarantees.

Several Indian companies showed that reported numbers can change, or do not match cash.
